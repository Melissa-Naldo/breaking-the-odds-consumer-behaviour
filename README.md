# Breaking the Odds: Consumer Behaviour Insights on Online Gambling

## Overview

Breaking the Odds is a qualitative consumer behaviour study exploring how online gambling has become normalised among young adults in Portugal.

The project examines how football culture, peer influence, advertising, digital payment systems and platform design shape betting behaviour and risk perception. It also evaluates whether technology-based interventions, such as smartwatch stress alerts, could support harm reduction during active betting sessions.

The study was developed for the Consumer Behaviour Insights course within the Master's in Data-Driven Marketing at NOVA IMS.

---

## Research Question

**How does cultural normalisation drive the high popularity of online gambling among young adults in Portugal?**

The research also explored how this normalisation influences:

- Betting behaviour
- Perceived financial and psychological risk
- Social acceptance
- Digital impulsivity
- Openness to harm-reduction technologies

---

## Background

Online gambling has shifted from a deliberate activity associated with physical venues to an immediate and constantly available digital experience.

Mobile betting applications, sports sponsorships, targeted advertising and frictionless payment methods have reduced the practical and psychological barriers traditionally associated with gambling.

In Portugal, this transition is reinforced by:

- A long-standing cultural familiarity with lotteries and games of chance
- The strong connection between football and betting
- Extensive brand visibility across sport and entertainment
- Continuous access through mobile applications
- Digital payment tools such as MBWay, Revolut and Apple Pay
- Social-media exposure and influencer promotion

The project investigates how these factors combine to make online betting feel ordinary, socially accepted and less risky.

## Methodology

The project used a qualitative, exploratory and interpretive research design.

### Participants

- 47 participants
- Ages 20 to 36
- Residents of Portugal
- Bettors and non-bettors included
- Approximately 40% women and 60% men
- Approximately balanced representation of active gamblers and non-gamblers

### Data Collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted remotely through Microsoft Teams.

The research process included:

- An 18-question interview script
- Interviews conducted in Portuguese and English
- Sessions lasting from approximately 15 minutes to one hour
- Audio and video recording with explicit participant consent
- Automated transcription followed by manual review
- Participant anonymisation
- Secure three-month data-retention procedures

### Sampling

A convenience and purposive sampling strategy was used, drawing participants from the researchers' personal, academic and professional networks.

### Analytical Approach

The interview transcripts were analysed through thematic coding.

The coding process linked:

- Research questions
- Participant IDs
- Repeated themes
- Keywords
- Supporting quotations
- Contradictory and outlier perspectives

The analysis combined inductive theme identification with a structured coding framework.

## Key Findings

### 1. Online Gambling Is Highly Normalised

The majority of participants perceived online gambling as either fully normalised or rapidly becoming normalised in Portugal.

Betting was described not as a niche or hidden behaviour, but as part of everyday digital and social culture.

### 2. Exposure Is Continuous and Multi-Channel

The most frequently mentioned sources of exposure were:

- Friends and peers
- Television and football broadcasts
- Targeted advertising
- Social media
- Public figures and influencers
- Sports sponsorships

Participants frequently described betting advertisements as unavoidable and present throughout ordinary entertainment consumption.

### 3. Exposure Often Begins Before Adulthood

Many participants reported first encountering gambling between the ages of 14 and 17.

Early exposure frequently occurred through:

- Family lotteries
- Scratch cards
- Euromillions
- Football-related betting
- Advertising
- Friends and social groups

This suggests that normalisation may begin before an individual makes a conscious decision to gamble.

### 4. Football Legitimises Betting

Football emerged as one of the strongest mechanisms of normalisation.

Forty-four of the 47 participants connected betting closely with football fandom.

Because football is culturally celebrated, betting associated with it is often perceived as:

- Entertainment
- A social ritual
- A demonstration of sports knowledge
- A more acceptable form of gambling

This framing creates a false distinction between sports betting and other games of chance.

### 5. Social Belonging Drives Participation

Peer influence was identified as a major factor in betting behaviour.

Participants described both:

- Direct encouragement from friends
- Indirect pressure through Fear of Missing Out

Wins were often shared publicly, while losses remained invisible. This created a distorted perception of profitability and reinforced the idea that betting was common and rewarding.

### 6. Digital Access Removes Psychological Friction

All participants identified digital payment systems as changing the psychological experience of spending money.

Tools such as MBWay, Revolut and Apple Pay reduce the visibility and emotional impact of financial loss.

Mobile applications further increase impulsivity through:

- Instant deposits
- Constant availability
- Push notifications
- Promotions
- Live in-play betting
- Default deposit amounts
- The absence of social observation

The digital environment turns betting from a deliberate action into a seamless habit.

### 7. Culture Can Mask Addiction

Participants identified several warning signs of problematic gambling:

- Trying to recover losses
- Borrowing money
- Betting with money needed for essential expenses
- Increasing frequency
- Betting on unfamiliar markets
- Loss of control
- Concealing losses

Despite recognising these risks, participants described betting as culturally familiar and commercially legitimised.

### 8. Biometric Alerts Alone Are Not Enough

Participants showed considerable scepticism toward smartwatch alerts and automated stress notifications.

Many believed these prompts would be ignored during active betting.

Financial-transparency tools were considered more useful, particularly:

- Cumulative loss summaries
- Spending limits
- Income-linked deposit controls
- Clear long-term financial reporting

These interventions may reintroduce the psychological friction removed by digital payment systems.

## Consumer Behaviour Insights

### Normalisation Precedes Active Participation

Repeated exposure through family practices, advertising, sport and social groups makes gambling feel familiar before the first bet is placed.

This reflects observational learning: individuals internalise behaviours by repeatedly seeing them modelled by trusted people and institutions.

### Football Creates a Risk-Shielding Effect

Betting inherits the social legitimacy of football.

Consumers may therefore interpret betting as an extension of fandom rather than as a financial-risk activity.

### Social Belonging Is a Stronger Entry Point Than Profit

The desire to participate in group conversations and shared rituals can be more influential than the expectation of financial gain.

### Digital Frictionlessness Encourages Impulsive Behaviour

When money becomes an abstract number on a screen, consumers experience less resistance before spending.

### Structural Measures May Be More Effective Than Individual Nudges

Participants expressed greater trust in regulation, financial transparency and advertising restrictions than in standalone technological reminders.

---

## Limitations

The findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations:

- Convenience sampling may introduce selection bias.
- Many participants were connected to university or professional networks.
- The sample is not representative of all young adults in Portugal.
- Gambling behaviour was self-reported.
- Social desirability may have influenced responses.
- The study is qualitative and cannot estimate population prevalence.
- The findings identify perceptions and patterns, not causal relationships.
